
Full life cycle oriented intelligent computing center location and configuration:  Considering green operation and output value contribution
Yongliang ZHANG1, Dahong SUN,*, Jianing ZHU1, Ruoxue YU1
(1. School of Management, China University of Mining and Technology-Beijing, Beijing 100083, China)
Abstract: In recent years, the surging demand for computing power has driven the rapid expansion of intelligent computing centers (ICCs). However, accompanying challenges have become increasingly prominent, including high construction costs, massive energy consumption, rising greenhouse gas emissions, and unbalanced regional economic development. Existing spatial planning is primarily cost-oriented, failing to internalize the environmental externality costs of carbon emissions and ignoring the macroeconomic contributions of ICCs as new infrastructure. To address these limitations, this study explores an expansion approach that balances environmental responsibility with macroeconomic contributions. Based on life cycle cost and life cycle assessment, this study incorporates the social cost of carbon into the evaluation system and proposes a life cycle socio-economic cost framework. Furthermore, this study develops a bi-objective location optimization model to integrate life cycle socio-economic costs and output value pulling effects. The Gurobi solver is employed to co-optimize decision variables such as ICC spatial layout and computing resource allocation. Taking China as a case study, empirical analyses are conducted under various scenarios of task demands, power supply structures, and economic growth targets. The results show that the proposed expansion scheme can reduce the total social cost of carbon of newly built ICCs by 2.4% while achieving a gross output value contribution share of 0.3087%. Future ICC expansion should prioritize Inner Mongolia, Shanxi, and Ningxia in the North, as well as Hubei, Yunnan, Fujian, and Sichuan in the South. This study offers a novel perspective on the green expansion of ICCs and their coordinated development with the regional economy.
Keywords: Intelligent computing center; Location optimization; Social cost of carbon; Output value pulling; Life cycle socio-economic cost
1. Introduction
Since 2010, the number of global Internet users has more than doubled, while the global Internet traffic has increased 20-fold [1]. This exponential growth has driven a substantial surge in energy consumption by data centers and data transmission networks, imposing significant environmental pressure. In 2022, the electricity consumption of the global data centers reached 460 TWh, accounting for approximately 2% of the global total electricity demand [2], and it is expected that this proportion will rise to 8% by 2030 [3]. This surge in energy consumption has brought significant pressure on carbon emissions. The total carbon dioxide equivalent generated by data centers and data transmission networks is 330 million tons, accounting for about 0.9% of global energy-related emissions [1]. With the rise of generative AI and Large Language Models (LLMs), Intelligent Computing Centers (ICCs) are increasingly replacing traditional data centers as the mainstream construction paradigm. ICCs are novel public computing infrastructures that leverage the latest AI theories and advanced AI computing architectures to provide the computing, data, and algorithmic services required by AI applications [4]. Unlike traditional data centers for general computing tasks, ICCs take high-power, high-density GPU clusters as their core computing engines. This structural shift not only intensifies hardware power density but also poses unprecedented challenges to power supply stability, cooling system efficiency, and carbon emission control [5]. Driven by the dual imperatives of carbon neutrality and digital economy transformation, it is imperative for data centers to expand in an energy-efficient and low-carbon manner [6]. Therefore, how to achieve coordinated development between green, low-carbon operations and regional economies while meeting the explosive demand for computing power has become a global challenge that urgently needs to be addressed.
Many factors influence the energy efficiency and carbon emissions of ICCs, but geographic location and computing power configuration strategies are critical [7, 8]. As high-load energy facilities, ICCs are highly sensitive to factors such as electricity prices, grid structures, PUE limits, cooling technologies, and water resource capacity [9]. First, regional differences in average temperature affect the choice of cooling technologies and allow for free cooling of IT components, thereby reducing the power demand of data centers [10]. Cooling systems account for approximately 40% of a data center’s total energy consumption, second only to IT servers [11]. Nordic countries have successfully attracted companies like Facebook and Amazon to build ICCs by leveraging abundant wind energy and cold climates. This effectively reduces cooling loads, achieving both cost control and carbon emission reduction [12]. Second, differences in regional grid structures determine the cleanliness of the energy supply. Guiding computing facilities to areas rich in renewable energy is considered an effective way to reduce carbon emissions and improve economic feasibility [13, 14]. Mas et al. proposed a new green and flexible multi-energy microgrid for ICCs, which effectively reduces operating costs and carbon emissions [15]. Finally, regarding the coupling of computing power and electricity, data loads are adjustable [16]. Therefore, power demand can be shifted across time and space through data scheduling between ICCs. This successfully alleviates power curtailment issues and reduces carbon emissions [8, 17, 18]. Guo et al. proposed a comprehensive management framework to optimize workload shifting and energy scheduling, reducing the operating costs of ICCs [19]. However, existing literature predominantly focuses on energy consumption optimization during the operational phase or carbon emissions accounting at the physical layer. Few studies examine the mathematical models of ICC costs and carbon emissions from a full life cycle perspective, and they fail to translate the negative externalities of carbon emissions into quantifiable economic losses. The socio-economic damage caused by global warming resulting from carbon emissions is the primary manifestation of its negative externalities, and the concept of the social cost of carbon (SCC) provides a quantitative description of these negative externalities [20]. Based on this, this paper introduces the Social Cost of Carbon to monetize the environmental impacts of carbon emissions, enabling a more intuitive assessment of the true economic losses incurred by high carbon emissions within cost-benefit analyses. This is crucial for formulating scientifically grounded emission reduction policies [21, 22].
ICCs not only bring substantial carbon emission pressure but also act as new infrastructure to stimulate regional economic development [23, 24]. From the perspective of social and urban studies, ICCs, as “visible network geography,” are reshaping regional economic structures and promoting resource sharing across regions [25-27]. Currently, mainstream location optimization models mostly treat ICCs as single cost centers, with objective functions usually set to minimize construction and operational costs. However, this perspective ignores the macroeconomic multiplier effects that ICCs possess as “new digital infrastructure.” In fact, the ICC not only amplifies regional resource and environmental pressures but also acts as a catalyst for industrial and economic growth. The establishment of ICCs can not only directly stimulate huge investments in upstream industries such as civil engineering, equipment manufacturing, and power facilities, but also empower downstream industries such as artificial intelligence, big data, and the Industrial Internet by providing computing power during the operational phase. Consequently, this exerts both pulling and driving effects on the upstream and downstream industrial chains [28]. Especially in the context of interconnected computing networks, establishing ICCs in economically underdeveloped yet energy-rich regions not only fosters closer collaboration across the industrial chain but also provides robust support for regional economic diversification. Thus, the planning of ICCs is not merely a single engineering construction issue, but evolves into a complex system engineering task that deeply couples energy systems, the climate environment, and the regional economy [29]. Unfortunately, existing mathematical models rarely quantify the economic multiplier effect of ICCs on regional or national economies, and there is an even greater lack of research incorporating macroeconomic contributions as core objective variables into location optimization frameworks. Therefore, how to minimize expansion costs while balancing green, low-carbon development with macroeconomic growth momentum represents a unique perspective that distinguishes this study from previous research.
Currently, few studies have monetized the environmental and economic impacts of ICCs or systematically investigated their location and configuration using mathematical optimization approaches. To address this research gap, the main contributions of this study are summarized as follows. First, develop a life cycle socio-economic cost (LCSC) framework to evaluate life cycle cost (LCC) and the total social cost of carbon (TSCC) from a full life cycle perspective. This framework provides a systematic tool for integrating economic costs with environmental externalities. Second, based on the proposed framework, incorporate an input–output model into the proposed framework to quantify the macroeconomic output effects induced by ICC investments, enabling a unified evaluation across economic, environmental, and cost dimensions. Finally, a location optimization model is proposed that simultaneously considers cost, economic, and environmental factors to identify optimal expansion pathways. Overall, the proposed location and configuration framework provides robust theoretical support and practical decision-making insights for achieving a coordinated balance between green operation and economic growth.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 elaborates on the core issues regarding the expansion of ICCs. Section 3 details the mathematical modeling process and solution methods. Section 4 introduces the data sources and processing procedures for the case study. Section 5 analyzes the results under different scenarios. Section 6 provides an in-depth discussion of the key findings and policy implications. Section 7 concludes the paper and proposes future research directions.
2. Key problem statement
In the complex decision-making process surrounding the expansion and resource allocation of ICCs, decision-makers are not faced with a simple location problem under a single objective. Rather, they must balance expansion costs, environmental responsibilities, and macroeconomic contributions while ensuring service quality, as illustrated in Table 1.
Table 1. Objectives of the intelligent computing center location and configuration
Objective
Main Content
Total life cycle cost
As capital-intensive infrastructures, ICCs incur massive LCC that dictate project financial viability, making it an absolute prerequisite [30].
Total social cost of carbon
As carbon neutrality targets approach, carbon emissions have shifted from mere externalities to internalized financial risks [31]. Potential carbon tax mechanisms will directly translate high emissions into exorbitant corporate costs [32]. Thus, proactively avoiding high-carbon grids is not only an environmental responsibility but also an economically rational strategy to mitigate future compliance risks.
Gross output value contribution share
Unlike traditional infrastructures, ICCs exhibit significant industrial spillover effects that stimulate related economic output. Beyond directly driving civil engineering and equipment investments, ICCs empower upstream and downstream industries via computing capabilities, generating a broader macroeconomic “multiplier effect” [33].
To achieve the above objectives, the following decisions must be considered in the layout design of ICCs. First, location and scale determination. Different regions exhibit significant heterogeneity in climate (affecting PUE and cooling), energy mix (emissions), and factor costs (land, labor, materials, and electricity) [34]. Because electricity dominates operational expenses, even minor price fluctuations are amplified by massive energy consumption, directly dictating financial viability [35]. Furthermore, location directly determines the trade-off between economic benefits and environmental costs. Building in western regions with abundant green power can significantly reduce carbon costs, but long-distance transmission costs are high. Conversely, while construction in developed regions offers a stronger input-output multiplier effect [36], it entails higher energy consumption and carbon emissions. Second, computing resource allocation decision. Task distribution must balance network latency and carbon emissions. Assigning computing tasks to local servers ensures low latency, but if the local power grid relies primarily on thermal power, this may lock in high carbon emissions. Conversely, cross-regional allocation to remote green ICCs effectively mitigates carbon emissions but increases network latency and infrastructure investments. Third, resource supply decision. Optimizing server provision enables the dynamic matching of computing demand with green power, thereby enhancing the national network’s overall resource efficiency. Server allocation and supply volume are key to balancing service quality and resource utilization [37]. In summary, scientifically coordinating location, scale, and resource configuration during the planning phase is imperative.
Based on the above analysis, the expansion of the ICC can be abstracted as a multi-objective optimization problem featuring highly coupled decisions on location and scale determination, computing resource allocation, and computing resource supply.
3. Methodology
3.1 Problem Description and System Framework
To better assess the combined costs of investment and environmental impacts associated with ICCs, this study follows the approach outlined by Chen et al. [38], employing LCC and life cycle assessment (LCA) methodologies, and incorporates the TSCC into the analysis. These three elements are used to construct the LCSC framework, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The LCSC framework essentially serves as a decision-support tool that monetizes micro-level engineering construction costs and macro-level climate impacts into a common unit.

Figure 1. The life cycle socio-economic Cost Framework of ICC 
This study abstracts the expansion and resource allocation of the ICC as a multi-dimensional constrained location optimization problem, aiming to identify the Pareto optimal solution for construction costs, carbon social costs, and macroeconomic benefits from a full life cycle perspective. As shown in Fig. 2, the system framework and transmission mechanism for the spatial expansion of ICCs are presented. This framework consists of three layers: the decision layer, the transmission mechanism layer, and the objective layer. It reveals how geographical heterogeneity influences the final objectives through resource and socio-economic pathways. Location and scale play a decisive role in achieving low-cost, green expansion and economic development.
At the national level, decision-making regarding the layout of ICCs should consider the balance between economic and environmental considerations. To enhance resource allocation efficiency, decision-makers must make simultaneous strategic decisions across two dimensions within a constrained solution space. As the central planner, the government needs to address two key aspects: First, determine the location and construction scale (xi,m) of the ICC, along with its thermal power proportion (αi). The value xi,m determines the number of ICCs of scale m to be constructed in province i. As a key factor influencing carbon emissions, the thermal power proportion αi should be proactively optimized at the system level to prioritize cleaner energy strategies. Second, determine the service network (yi,j) and computational resource allocation (di,j) for the ICC. yi,j determines whether province i provides computational services to province j and decides the server allocation di,j. Specifically, location and scale are core factors influencing infrastructure construction costs and economic development. Meanwhile, the proportion of renewable energy in the grid power affects the green operation and economic viability of ICCs. Furthermore, the construction of the computing service network and the rational allocation of computing resources serve as effective means to improve resource allocation efficiency.

3.2.3 Total social cost of carbon
To mitigate the ecological damage caused by carbon emissions, decision-makers need to monetize the harm resulting from such emissions, which we define as the Total social cost of carbon. Therefore, it introduces the concept of the SCC to quantitatively measure and evaluate the economic losses resulting from the impact of carbon dioxide emissions on climate change. This is of significant importance for the formulation of climate change policies [22].
To accurately quantify the environmental externalities of the expansion of the ICC, this study draws on the SCC estimation model referenced in Wang et al. [40]. This model shares a similar structure with the integrated assessment model, comprising a climate module, a loss function module, and a discount rate module [41, 42]. This study forecasts the dynamically increasing SCC for each province on an annual basis under the SSP2-6.0 pathway, as shown in Eq. (20). 3.4.3 Model transformation and solution
Due to the construction of ICCs facing quantity constraints, the output value driving effect cannot increase indefinitely. To maximize this economic driving effect while minimizing the LCSC, we must balance these two aspects. Therefore, this study adopts the ϵ-constraint method to set the output value contribution as a restriction. This approach converts the problem into a single objective format targeting the LCSC [46], as illustrated in Eq. (40). After transforming the proposed ICC expansion model into its equivalent single-objective form, the model solution and result discussion scheme is designed, as shown in Fig. 3.
Data preparation process. In this study, after data collection is complete, data preprocessing is performed to obtain intermediate variables such as hi,j and yi,j,i≠j, thereby ensuring that all data can be directly used for preliminary calculations. The precalculation aims to obtain the lower bound Mmin of the gross output value contribution share. This is achieved by solving Eq. (40) without the constraint on output value contribution, i.e., by solving a problem subject to Eqs. (31)–(39) under the constraint of minimizing economic costs.
Model Solution Process. This study employs Python to manage data processing, computation, and result output. To investigate the optimal expansion scheme for ICCs, four values are set for the economic development attitude parameter (ψ=1, ψ=1.1, ψ=1.2, ψ=1.3) . Additionally, six propagation delay scenarios τ=1ms,  τ=3ms,  τ=5ms,  τ=7ms,  τ=9ms,  τ=11ms and five power supply mix scenarios （α=0，α=0.25，α=0.5，α=0.75，α=1） are designed. In the aforementioned 15 scenarios, the Gurobi solver is used to solve Eq. (40) to obtain the values of the decision variables and the objective function. The computations are performed on a 64-bit system equipped with a 12th Gen Intel Core i5-12600KF CPU @ 3.70Ghz and 32 GB RAM, with computation times ranging from 7 ms to 15 ms per scenario. Upon solving the model, the results regarding the spatial layout, associated costs, and gross output value contribution shares of the ICCs are illustrated in Figs. 4-10. Finally, two hypothetical scenarios are designed to investigate the impacts of the power supply mix and electricity prices in different regions on construction costs and gross output value contribution shares, as illustrated in Figs. 12 and 13, respectively.
Discussion Process. To achieve a feasible gross output value contribution share and an acceptable propagation delay in the ICC’s expansion problem, three sets of sensitivity analyses are designed. First, 24 sub-scenarios were proposed. By integrating economic development attitude parameters and six transmission delay values, feasible output value pull effects were screened through comparing the results of relevant costs and gross output value contribution shares, as shown in Fig. 14. Second, 36 sub-scenarios are established by integrating six economic development parameter values with six propagation delay values. By comparing the associated costs and gross output value contribution shares, as shown in Fig. 15, the acceptable propagation delay is determined. Each scenario is solved by varying the values of ψ, τ, and αi individually, with computation times ranging from 7s to 20s. Based on the results of the first two groups of analyses, the optimal combination scheme that balances economic pulling benefits and propagation delay performance is selected, and the reasonable value ranges for ψ and τ are determined. On this basis, a third set of hypothetical scenarios is constructed to further evaluate the impact of the energy structure of the ICC on costs, the environment, and economic pulling effects, as shown in Fig. 16. All scenarios are simulated based on actual geographical distribution and load demand data to ensure that the results possess practical guiding significance.

5. Result
In this section, we refer to existing literature to set numerical values for parameters, including the gross output value contribution share constraint (ψ), propagation delay (τ), and power supply structure (α) [12, 45]. We also present the results under four scenarios of gross output value contribution share constraints, six scenarios of propagation delay, and five scenarios of power supply structure.
By varying the policy preference coefficient ψ, a series of nondominated solutions is generated to finally form a Pareto front. This provides decision-makers with a range of cost-benefit trade-offs. As shown in Fig. 4, as the attitude parameter ψ increases, the marginal LCSC required to achieve the gross output value contribution share increment climbs sharply. When ψ∈ [1.0, 1.1], the LCSC increases from 5488.3 billion CNY to 5528.0 billion CNY, which is an increase of only 39.7 billion CNY and a growth rate of about 0.72%. When ψ∈ [1.1, 1.2], the LCSC increases by 124.9 billion CNY with a growth rate of 2.28%. When ψ∈ [1.2, 1.3], the LCSC increases significantly by 225.0 billion CNY with a growth rate of 4.1%. When ψ∈ [1.3, 1.75], the LCSC surges from 5877.8 billion CNY to 7593.8 billion CNY, showing a massive increase of 1716.0 billion CNY and a high growth rate of 29.2%.
The analysis shows that ψ∈1.0,1.3 is the optimal decision tradeoff region that decision-makers should focus on. Particularly in the value range from 1.0 to 1.1, the system only needs to sacrifice less than 1% of the LCSC to leverage an increase of up to 1.24 trillion CNY in the lower limit of the macroeconomic output value. Within the ψ∈1.3,1.7 interval, to obtain the same unit of output value increment, the average marginal cost paid by the system is at least 8 times that of the initial stage.

5.4 Sensitivity and Robustness Analysis
Considering the context of uncertain market environments and technological transformations, this section selects electricity price fluctuations and changes in the α for perturbation analysis.
5.4.1 Electricity price fluctuation
Energy costs dominate the LCSC of ICCs. It is crucial to study the impact of electricity price fluctuations on the layout and various costs. We established three regions consisting of the East, Central, and West. We simulate two scenarios, including a 1% thermal power price increase due to fossil energy scarcity and a 1% green electricity price decrease driven by technological progress.
Fig. 12 shows that when the green electricity price decreases, the LCC and the gross output value contribution share in the East, Central, and West regions decrease compared to the baseline scenario. Specifically, in the scenario where the Western green electricity price decreases by 1%, the LCSC drops to the lowest among all scenarios at 4.411 trillion CNY. This represents a 1.44% decrease from the baseline scenario and makes it the optimal cost plan. However, the gross output value contribution share under this scenario also drops to the lowest value of 0.2823%. 
Regarding carbon emission reduction, the data indicate that the scenario with a 1% increase in the western thermal power price achieves the best decarbonization effect. Under this scenario, the TSCC falls to 1.0189 trillion CNY to show a decrease of about 0.42% compared to the baseline scenario. Meanwhile, the computing power scale in the West slightly decreases by 16,000 units. This demonstrates that moderately increasing the western thermal power price can effectively curb the blind expansion of high carbon loads. This promotes the structural optimization of the computing power layout to achieve the optimal global decarbonization effect with a relatively small construction transfer cost. 
Regarding the spatial construction layout of ICCs, different regions show significant differences in their sensitivity to electricity price fluctuations. Furthermore, the Central region plays a critical geographical buffering and load substitution role. When the eastern thermal power price rises by 1%, the computing power scale in the East is significantly compressed. The newly added racks drop sharply from the baseline of 236,000 units to 200,000 units to mark a 15.2% decline. The overflowing computing power demand is mainly absorbed by the Central region, where 528,000 units are built. Conversely, when the eastern green electricity price drops by 1%, the eastern computing power scale rebounds to 256,000 units with an 8.5% increase. When the central thermal power price increases by 1%, the Central region loses its computing power cost advantage. The newly added racks shrink to 456,000 units with an 8.8% drop. This forces a portion of the computing power investment to flow backward into the East and increases the eastern scale to 260,000 units. When the central green electricity price decreases by 1%, the central scale slightly increases to 503,000 units. This accurately absorbs the spillover demand from the East. This fully proves that the Central region utilizes its moderate geographical location and power grid structure to display a strong factor substitution effect in the national computing power network. When prices in surrounding regions are unfavorable, it can quickly meet the demands by moving west or east. When its own prices fluctuate, it can also form a highly flexible construction exchange mechanism with the East and the West.
The analysis reveals a significant asymmetry in the sensitivity of different regions to energy prices. Reducing green power prices in the Eastern region represents the solution with the lowest total social operating cost. In contrast, merely lowering green power prices in the Western region may induce blind capacity expansion, thereby driving up total carbon emissions [59].

5.4.2 Regional power mix change
To further explore the marginal driving effect of the regional grid greening process on computing network layouts, we simulated three perturbation scenarios based on the previous analysis, where the green electricity share in the eastern, central, and western regional grids increased by 1%, respectively. 
As shown in Fig. 13, when the green electricity share in the eastern region increases by 1%, reverse construction occurs among regions. The total number of new racks in the eastern region increases from the baseline of 236,000 to 256,000, while the central region correspondingly decreases from 500,000 to 479,000. This indicates that the increase in the eastern green electricity share reduces the TSCC by over 54.2 billion CNY, reshaping its power structure advantage and thereby diverting some computing power investment from the central region to the eastern region. Because the eastern region is the computing load center, this causes the LCSC to drop by approximately 26.9 billion CNY and the TSCC to decrease by 5%.
When the green electricity share in the central region increases by 1%, the spatial layout also undergoes significant adjustments. The number of new racks in the central region slightly increases from 500,000 to 505,000, while the eastern and western regions decrease by 20,000 and 4,000, respectively. This further confirms the siphon effect of regional green electricity advantages on computing power capital. Under this scenario, the LCSC drops to the lowest point among the three perturbation scenarios, but the macroeconomic driving effect also declines, bringing the gross output value contribution share down to 0.2852%. 
In contrast, when the green electricity share in the western region increases by 1%, the spatial layout of the computing network remains unchanged, and the number of new racks in the eastern, central, and western regions is exactly the same as the baseline scenario. Although the physical layout remains unchanged, the green electricity carbon reduction dividend under the massive computing base in the western region leads to significant reductions in both the LCSC and the TSCC. Furthermore, it achieves positive growth in the macroeconomic driving effect, and the gross output value contribution share rises against the trend from the baseline of 0.2865% to 0.2869%. This profoundly indicates that continuously deepening the energy structure transformation in the western region can achieve a mutually beneficial outcome for environmental emission reduction and economic driving benefits without paying the price of drastic spatial reconstruction.

6. Discussion
This section investigates scientifically feasible and acceptable solutions for propagation latency, the TSCC, and economic development.
6.1 Acceptable gross output value contribution share
This section aims to determine the acceptable gross output value contribution share constraint for national planners regarding ICCs. The results, combining four gross output value contribution share constraint scenarios (ψ=1, ψ=1.1, ψ=1.2, ψ=1.3) and six propagation latency scenarios (1 ms, 3 ms, 5 ms, 7 ms, 9 ms, 11 ms), are presented in Fig. 14.
According to the orange line in Fig. 14(f), the gross output value contribution share sits at 0.2808% when the propagation delay falls below 1 ms. We treat this as Mmin which corresponds to ψ=1. Making the gross output value contribution share constraint stricter by raising ψ from 1 to 1.1, 1.2, and 1.3 drives up the corresponding LCSC. Specifically, the cost climbs from 5462.3 billion CNY to 5504.2 billion CNY, 5638.4 billion CNY, and 5868.9 billion CNY, respectively. Still, the cost growth rates vary across these stages. Increasing the gross output value contribution share constraint by 10% (ψ=1.1) pushes the LCSC up by 0.77%. Increasing the constraint by 20% (ψ=1.2) triggers a 3.22% increase in the LCSC. Finally, increasing the constraint by 30% (ψ=1.3) generates a 7.44% increase in the total cost.
A comparison of Figs. 14 (a) – (f) reveals that, for each transmission delay scenario, a 10% increase in the initial share of total output results in the lowest increase and growth rate in LCSC. For example, when the propagation delay is less than 5 ms, the first 10% increase results in a 0.71% growth rate in LCSC. A subsequent 10% increase (i.e., a cumulative 20% increase) leads to a marginal increase of 2.29% in LCSC, which is 3.2 times the initial increase. A third 10% increase would result in a cost growth rate of 7.13%, meaning that a third 10% increase in the share of total output value would lead to a marginal increase of 4.13% in the LCSC. The same cost growth trend can be observed in other propagation delay scenarios. Excessively pursuing extremely high output value contributions will cause LCSC to rise exponentially, thereby severely undermining economic benefits.
Based on the above analysis, for decision-makers in the ICC industry, controlling the attitude parameter within 10% when planning the location and design of new centers is more economically feasible.

6.2 Acceptable propagation delay
This section aims to determine which propagation latency scenario is more feasible in terms of both cost and environmental impact. We established six propagation latency scenarios (1 ms, 3 ms, 5 ms, 7 ms, 9 ms, and 11 ms) and six different gross output value contribution share constraints (ψ=1, ψ=1.02， ψ=1.04, ψ=1.06, ψ=1.08, ψ=1.1), resulting in a total of 36 sub-scenarios, as illustrated in Fig. 15.
Fig. 15 displays the range of changes in the LCSC, the LCC, and the TSCC across the aforementioned scenarios. This study employs standardized processing and marginal analysis to provide a clearer view of the fluctuations. According to the purple line tracking the LCSC, the sharpest decline occurs between 1 ms and 5 ms across the six propagation delay scenarios, with a decrease of at least 1.7%. The marginal drop between 5 ms and 11 ms stays within 0.2%. The blue line clearly shows that the trend in LCC is similar to that of LCSC. Based on the green line, the TSCC represents 20.1% of the initial LCSC when the propagation delay falls below 1 ms. When the propagation delay expands from 1 ms to 11 ms in 2 ms increments, the TSCC first decreases and then stabilizes to reach its absolute minimum at either 5 ms or 7 ms. Comparing Figs. 15(a)-13(f) comprehensively uncovers that carbon emissions are minimized at a propagation delay of 5 ms or 7 ms under every gross output value contribution share constraint. Furthermore, the marginal decreases for the LCSC and the LCC are significantly larger within the first 5 ms than in the 5 ms to 11 ms range.
Based on the above analysis, for decision-makers in the ICC industry, controlling the propagation latency within 5 during location and design is an economically acceptable choice that achieves minimum TSCC while ensuring a high quality of service.

6.3 Acceptable power mix
This section aims to analyze how the power supply structure changes to enable the expansion of the intelligent computing center to have a green impact. This study defines six power supply scenarios (α=1, α=0.75, α=0.5, α=0.25, α=0) and six different constraints on the gross output value contribution share (ψ=1, ψ=1.02, ψ=1.04, ψ=1.06, ψ=1.08, ψ=1.1), resulting in a total of 36 sub-scenarios.
Fig. 16 illustrates the variations in the LCSC, LCC, TSCC, and gross output value contribution share for the ICC under the different scenarios described above (ψ= 1 to 1.1). In terms of LCSC, across all output contribution scenarios, costs decrease significantly as the share of thermal power α decreases. When the share of thermal power α∈[0,0.25], the marginal reduction in LCSC is greatest, at approximately 14.6%. When the share of thermal power α∈[0.25,1], the marginal reduction gradually slows and remains between 8.1% and 12%. Observations of the LCC reveal that its variation trend is similar to that of the LCSC. It also presents the most obvious decrease in the α∈[0,0.25], interval with a drop of over 5.3%. It’s marginal decline then gradually narrows. The drastic changes in environmental costs can be clearly observed from the TSCC. As α∈0.25,1 is gradually reduced, the marginal rate of decline in the TSCC remains relatively moderate (with the reduction staying around 30%). However, once the share of thermal power is further reduced from 25% to a scenario of complete reliance on green electricity, the TSCC decreases sharply and significantly. In the ψ=1 scenario, the TSCC plummets from 600 billion CNY to approximately 380.9 million CNY. In terms of gross output value contribution share, the marginal decline in gross output value contribution share is actually smaller within the interval α∈0,0.25 than within the interval α∈0.25,1.
Therefore, for decision-makers in the ICC industry, it is recommended that the share of thermal power be kept below 25% when planning the location and energy system design for new ICCs. This strategy not only significantly reduces the TSCC but also falls within the optimal range in terms of overall life cycle economics, making it an ideal solution that balances economic development with the dual carbon goals.

6.4 Core findings
During the expansion of China’s ICC industry, by reasonably planning the layout, scale, and energy structure of these centers, it is possible to reduce the TSCC by at least 2.4% and achieve a gross output value contribution share of 0.3087% (ψ=1.1). Under the actual power supply structure, Fig. 14 shows that an attitude parameter of 10% for the gross output value contribution share is more cost-effective. Fig. 15 indicates that a time delay of ≤5 ms is optimal. Furthermore, Fig. 15(f) reveals that, under the actual power supply structure, when the attitude parameter for the gross output value contribution share, ψ=1.1, the TSCC are 20.1%, 17.9%, 17.7%, 17.6%, 17.6%, and 17.5%, respectively. Therefore, when the time delay is ≤5 ms, the TSCC can be reduced by at least 2.4%.
Given current real-world provincial power supply data and transmission technology levels, setting a data service propagation delay of 5 ms as the threshold for determining the geographic location of ICCs can keep expansion costs, TSCC and economic growth within acceptable ranges while achieving good service quality. This conclusion can be drawn from Fig. 15, which illustrates and compares the relative values of LCSC, TSCC, and economic growth under different propagation delay scenarios. In each scenario (ψ=1、1.02、1.04、1.06、1.08、1.1), when propagation latency increases from 1 ms to 5 ms, all types of costs show a significant downward trend, with a reduction of approximately 3.6%. However, when propagation latency continues to increase from 5 ms to 7 ms, 9 ms, and 11 ms, the reduction in all types of costs drops to 0.1%. This indicates that relaxing latency constraints is an effective means of reducing TSCC, but the significant marginal cost-reduction effect gradually diminishes. Therefore, a propagation delay of 5 ms can keep the LCSC within an acceptable range.
From the green perspective of the ICC industry, reducing the thermal power proportion in the power grid to below 0.25 represents a highly economical choice. This conclusion can be drawn from multiple angles. The power supply structure scenario analysis in Fig. 9(b) reveals that increasing the green electricity penetration rate can significantly lower the LCSC, the LCC, the TSCC, and the national industrial driving effect. However, the marginal reductions in the various costs reach their peak when the thermal power proportion is above 0.25. These reductions subsequently fall back in the pure thermal power scenario. A comprehensive comparison of Fig. 16(a) through Fig. 16(f) uncovers further details. Keeping the thermal power proportion below 25% under any gross output value contribution share constraint scenario can maintain the TSCC within a low baseline range. Furthermore, it effectively curbs the marginal growth trend of the LCC and the marginal reduction trend of the LCSC.
Regarding the future geographical distribution of ICCs, it is recommended to focus on Inner Mongolia, Shaanxi, and Ningxia in the north; Hubei in the central region; Yunnan and Sichuan in the south; and Liaoning and Heilongjiang in the northeast. In the north, establishing construction hubs in Inner Mongolia and Ningxia can increase the share of renewable energy and reduce carbon emissions [12, 60]. In the central region, Hubei, Sichuan, and Yunnan account for a significant share of construction, as these three provinces rank among the top three nationally in terms of the proportion of hydropower in their energy mix and rank second in terms of their contribution to clean energy generation [45]. This finding is evident in Fig. 5, which illustrates the location and distribution of ICCs under different propagation delay scenarios. It can be observed that as the propagation delay varies between 1 ms, 3 ms, 5 ms, 7 ms, 9 ms, and 11 ms, the layout of ICCs gradually shifts from a dispersed to a concentrated pattern. As discussed earlier, a propagation delay of 5 ms can keep the LCSC and the gross output value contribution share within an acceptable range; therefore, we further analyze the location of ICCs in Fig. 5(c). The results show that only 11 provinces need to undertake the construction of ICCs. Among them, Hubei Province in Central China bears 24% of the task, ranking first. Inner Mongolia, Ningxia, Shanxi, and Shaanxi in North China bear 22% of the task. Yunnan and Sichuan bear 32% of the task, and Liaoning and Heilongjiang in Northeast China bear 11.5% of the task. Consequently, these provinces require special attention during the expansion of ICCs.
It should be noted that there are certain discrepancies between the actual situation and the findings discussed above. This is primarily because this study is based on real-world data and a scientific analysis of the expansion of the ICC industry. However, in China, local governments have the authority to guide the development of specific industries through targeted policy directives and requirements. Such policy-driven approaches result in a certain deviation between the actual situation and the optimal state. For example, Guizhou Province has now become one of the leading provinces in the development of China’s ICC industry.
6.5 Policy implications
Based on the aforementioned multidimensional collaborative optimization model and scenario simulation results, the following targeted policy implications are proposed to achieve the coordinated development of green, low-carbon, and economically efficient national computing power networks:
For the central government, the multidimensional collaborative optimization model proposed in this study can serve as a quantitative decision-making tool for formulating the top-level design of the national computing power network. The model results indicate that, under current technological conditions, a network latency of 5 ms is the critical physical threshold for balancing LCSC and TSCC, with a gross output value contribution share reaching 0.3087% (the scenario with the highest relative ratio of Gross output value contribution share increase). Therefore, it is recommended that the government use this threshold to establish a tiered spatial layout system. For high-frequency inference-based operations, deployment in central regions like Hubei is encouraged. For training-based operations with extremely high latency tolerance, these may be directed to deep green power hinterlands in Southwest and North China, such as Sichuan, Yunnan, and Inner Mongolia, to avoid uncontrolled network transmission costs resulting from an excessive pursuit of low electricity prices. Furthermore, this study finds that a low-carbon orientation may weaken macroeconomic momentum. It is therefore recommended that the finance department establish a cross-provincial fiscal transfer payment system for computing power. Through tax revenue sharing or carbon credit trading, a proportion of the value-added tax generated by computing power usage in the eastern regions should be returned to the locations of ICCs in the western regions. These funds should be earmarked for enhancing the supporting capabilities of the digital industrial chain in the western regions, thereby artificially increasing the input-output multiplier in those areas. This approach would resolve the spatial conflict between resource allocation and economic development while pursuing low-carbon goals.
For Energy and Data Industry Regulators. Breaking the structural rigidity of regional layouts requires the implementation of differentiated market incentive policies. The study finds that maintaining the share of thermal power in the grid within the 0 to 0.25 range is the optimal interval for balancing low-carbon objectives and economic growth. To guide the formation of this structure, it is recommended to implement an asymmetric pricing strategy: reducing green power prices in the East and controlling thermal power prices in the West. In western regions like Inner Mongolia and Xinjiang, high-carbon emission spaces should be restricted via carbon taxes or price thresholds to force the consumption of clean energy. In eastern regions like Jiangsu, the focus should be on reducing grid wheeling charges and institutional costs for green power trading. Meanwhile, efforts should be made to establish a unified national collaborative trading market for computing power and green electricity, converting the resource advantages of the West into electricity price advantages for end-users through market-oriented means.
For ICC Enterprises and Investors. Enterprises and investors must reconstruct their project value evaluation systems while pursuing economic benefits. This study verifies that rational planning enables enterprises to deliver a significant 2.4% reduction in TSCC at the cost of only a minimal 0.71% rise in LCSC. Therefore, it is recommended that enterprises prioritize the “source-grid-load-storage” integration model at the micro-level location and operation. By utilizing wind and solar supporting facilities within the project boundary, they can avoid the stacking of transmission and distribution prices from the main grid. Furthermore, enterprises should proactively adopt a life cycle dual-constraint evaluation system integrating Power Usage Effectiveness and TSCC to replace the single PUE metric. This approach avoids the construction of low-PUE ICCs in regions dominated by thermal power.
7. Conclusions and future study
As the foundational cornerstone supporting the digital economy and a typical high-energy-consuming infrastructure, the scientific location and resource allocation of ICCs directly determine whether regional macroeconomic growth and national dual carbon targets can be effectively balanced. However, existing spatial layout studies often remain confined to single-stage construction and operational cost accounting. These studies generally ignore the comprehensive consideration of environmental externalities and macroeconomic impacts. To fill this theoretical gap, our study evaluates the total LCC and the total social cost of carbon based on a life cycle assessment. We subsequently established the LCSC metric. Based on this newly proposed framework, we designed a dual objective location optimization model that encompasses both the LCSC and the output value effect. Our research seamlessly integrates the LCC and the life cycle assessment with the dynamic social cost of carbon and multi-objective optimization. This comprehensive integration effectively overcomes the limitations of traditional isolated factor analysis. 
This study aims to optimize the location and resource allocation plan for the expansion of the ICC, with the goal of maximizing its economic contribution while minimizing both the LCSC and the TSCC. A multi-scenario empirical analysis was conducted on the allocation of computing power resources across 30 provinces in China. The findings indicate that: (1) Through rational planning of the location, layout, scale, and energy structure of newly constructed ICCs, China can achieve at least a 2.4% reduction in TSCC while contributing 0.3087% to the gross output value contribution share. (2) Given the current provincial power supply data and transmission technology levels, using a data service propagation latency of 5 ms as the threshold for determining locations can keep the LCSC, TSCC, and economic growth values within an acceptable range, while ensuring high Quality of Service. (3) Comprehensively considering the energy mix as well as planning and operational costs, future expansion of ICCs in China should prioritize deployments in Inner Mongolia, Shanxi, and Ningxia in the North; Hubei, Yunnan, Fujian, and Sichuan in the South; and Liaoning in the Northeast. (4) The findings indicate that reducing the thermal power share in the grid to below 0.25 offers the best cost-effectiveness.
Future research may be expanded in the following areas: First, the dynamic impact of large-scale infrastructure investment on regional industrial structure upgrading will be examined to refine macroeconomic benefit assessments. Second, node processing delays and queueing delays will be incorporated into the optimization framework to construct a more precise latency model. Third, local governments’ attitudes toward the intelligent computing center industry will be integrated into the optimization framework. ADDIN NE.Bib
